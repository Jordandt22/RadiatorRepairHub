A radiator cap is a pressure valve. The spring inside holds the cooling system at a set PSI so coolant can run hotter without boiling. A weak or wrong cap can overflow the tank, collapse a hose, or let the engine overheat even when the radiator looks fine.

These steps are general information, not a repair procedure for your car. If you're unsure, the engine is hot, or a new cap doesn't stop the coolant loss, take it to a radiator shop.

## Match The Cap Before You Buy

Read the PSI rating on the old cap or in the owner's manual. A 16 psi cap doesn't belong on a system designed for 13, and a 13 doesn't belong on a system designed for 16. Closed cooling systems that fill at a pressurized tank use a different cap than an open radiator neck. The cap and the neck have to be the same style.

Also look at the sealing surface. Nicks, rust, or a warped plastic neck will leak even with a new cap.

## How To Replace It

1. Wait until the engine is completely cool. Do not open a hot cap.
2. Compare the new cap's pressure rating and style to the old one and the manual.
3. Twist the old cap off slowly. If you hear a hiss while the engine is cool, stop and let it sit longer.
4. Wipe the neck. Install the new cap until it locks.
5. If coolant spilled or the level was low, top off with the coolant spec in the manual and bleed air if the system was opened. A spill-free funnel helps with that.
6. After a drive and a full cool-down, check for overflow and confirm the temp gauge stays normal.

## When A New Cap Is Not Enough

A cap won't seal a cracked tank, a split hose, a failing water pump, or a head gasket. If the system still won't hold pressure, get a cooling-system pressure test instead of pouring in stop leak.

Shops often replace the cap during a hose job or a flush because the part is cheap next to an overheat. If you've already swapped the cap and coolant is still disappearing, [find a shop](/search) and ask them to test the system and the cap separately.
