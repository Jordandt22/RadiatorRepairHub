When the temperature gauge climbs into the red, or steam comes from under the hood, your cooling system is not keeping up. Keep driving and you can warp a head or blow a gasket. Pull over, shut it down, and sort it out before that happens.

## What to do immediately

1. **Pull over safely** and turn off the engine.
2. **Do not open the radiator cap** while the engine is hot. Pressurized coolant can spray and burn you.
3. **Wait until the engine is fully cool** before checking anything. After a bad overheat, that can mean 30 minutes or longer. Never open a pressurized cap while the system is hot.
4. **Call for a tow** if the gauge stays in the red, you see steam, or you are far from help.

If you are tempted to nurse it a few more miles, read [Can You Drive With a Radiator Leak?](/blogs/can-you-drive-with-a-radiator-leak). The same caution applies whenever the engine is running hot.

## Common causes of overheating

### Low coolant or a leak

Coolant carries heat away from the engine. A leak in the radiator, a hose, or the water pump means less fluid and less cooling. Look for puddles, a sweet smell, or a dropping reservoir level. See [How to Spot a Radiator Leak](/blogs/how-to-spot-a-radiator-leak) for warning signs, or [Coolant Leak Under Car: Causes and What to Do](/blogs/coolant-leak-under-car-causes-and-what-to-do) if you are trying to pin down the source.

### Failed thermostat

The thermostat regulates coolant flow. If it sticks closed, coolant cannot circulate and the engine can overheat within minutes of driving. Unsure whether it is the thermostat or the radiator? Read [Thermostat vs Radiator: What's Causing Overheating?](/blogs/thermostat-vs-radiator-whats-causing-overheating).

### Clogged or damaged radiator

Rust, debris, or internal blockages cut the radiator's ability to shed heat. Road debris can also punch holes or bend fins enough to cause leaks and overheating.

### Broken water pump

The water pump keeps coolant moving. A worn impeller or leaking pump seal can stop circulation and trigger rapid overheating.

### Head gasket failure

Coolant can leak internally into the engine, so you overheat without an obvious puddle. White exhaust smoke or milky oil are red flags. More on that in [Radiator Leak vs Head Gasket Leak](/blogs/radiator-leak-vs-head-gasket-leak).

### Cooling fan not working

Electric fans should kick on at idle or in traffic. A bad fan motor, relay, or sensor can overheat you in stop-and-go even when highway speeds feel fine. See [Radiator Fan Not Working: Symptoms and Repair](/blogs/radiator-fan-not-working).

### Old or contaminated coolant

Neglected fluid loses its anti-corrosion properties and can clog the system. A [radiator flush](/blogs/radiator-flush-what-to-expect-and-cost) can help as maintenance, but it will not fix an active leak or a failed part.

## Signs you should not ignore

Review [7 Signs Your Radiator Needs Repair](/blogs/7-signs-your-radiator-needs-repair) for a full checklist. The biggest ones:

- Temperature gauge in the red zone
- Steam from under the hood
- Coolant loss with no clear explanation
- Heater blowing cold air at idle
- Repeated overheating after adding coolant

## How a shop diagnoses overheating

A tech will usually:

1. Pressure-test the cooling system for external leaks
2. Check thermostat operation and fan function
3. Inspect the water pump, hoses, and radiator
4. Test for head gasket failure if an internal leak looks likely

Start with a [radiator repair service](/category/radiator-repair-service) or [radiator shop](/category/radiator-shop) that sees cooling jobs regularly. General [auto repair shops](/category/auto-repair-shop) can help too if they have the right equipment.

## Prevention tips

- Check coolant level monthly when the engine is cold
- Follow your manufacturer's flush and service intervals
- Fix small leaks before they turn into overheating events
- Watch the temperature gauge in summer traffic and when towing
