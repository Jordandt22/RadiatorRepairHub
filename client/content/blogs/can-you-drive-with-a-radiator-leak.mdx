It depends on how bad the leak is and whether the engine is already running hot. Usually you should limit driving and get the cooling system checked soon. If you are not sure you have a leak yet, start with [How to Spot a Radiator Leak](/blogs/how-to-spot-a-radiator-leak).

## When you should not drive

**Stop and pull over safely if you notice any of these:**

- Temperature gauge in the red or warning light on
- Steam coming from under the hood
- Strong sweet smell of coolant inside or outside the car
- Coolant pouring out quickly (a large puddle forming while parked or driving)
- Engine running rough, losing power, or making unusual noises

Keep going in those conditions and you can end up with **warped heads, blown head gaskets, or a dead engine**. That bill dwarfs fixing a leak early.

## When a short drive might be okay

A **very slow seep** (a small damp spot, not a steady drip) might allow a careful trip to a nearby shop, but only if:

1. The temperature gauge stays in the normal range
2. You have enough coolant in the reservoir (check when the engine is cold)
3. The drive is short and you can watch the gauge the entire time
4. You are not towing, climbing hills, or sitting in heavy stop-and-go traffic

Even then, treat it as a one-time exception. Leaks usually get worse.

## Risks of driving with a leak

- **Low coolant:** Less heat gets carried away from the engine
- **Air in the system:** Hot spots and uneven cooling
- **Overheating:** Damages seals, gaskets, and cylinder heads
- **Breakdown:** You may stall in traffic or far from help

Coolant also helps prevent corrosion inside the engine. Running low for too long can hurt the water pump, hoses, and other parts even if you do not overheat right away.

## What to do before you drive anywhere

1. **Let the engine cool completely** before opening the radiator cap or reservoir.
2. **Check coolant level** in the overflow tank. Only top off when cold, and use the correct coolant type when you can. Plain water is an emergency stopgap only. It dilutes corrosion and freeze protection until you can refill properly.
3. **Look for the source:** hose, radiator seam, water pump weep hole, or cap. Also see [Radiator Cap Symptoms & Replacement](/blogs/radiator-cap-symptoms-and-replacement).
4. **Plan the shortest route** to a [radiator repair shop](/category/radiator-repair-service) or [radiator shop](/category/radiator-shop) and avoid hard acceleration.
5. **Bring coolant** and a tow number. If the gauge climbs, stop and get a tow instead of topping off on the roadside all afternoon.

If you are unsure, **call a tow truck**. It is cheaper than replacing an engine. Skip temporary chemical sealants unless a tech recommends one for a specific limp-home situation. Read [Is Radiator Stop Leak Safe?](/blogs/is-radiator-stop-leak-safe).

## How radiator shops can help

A specialist can pressure-test the system, find the exact leak, and tell you whether you need a hose, radiator, cap, or something bigger. Catch a small leak early and it is often a simple fix instead of a roadside emergency. Local [auto repair shops](/category/auto-repair-shop) that do cooling work can help too.
